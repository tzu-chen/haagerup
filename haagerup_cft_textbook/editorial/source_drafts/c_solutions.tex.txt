\section{Solutions for the lattice and construction chapters}

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-lattice-1}: cocycle signs}
Let $B(a,b)$ be the exponent in the cocycle, considered modulo two.
The diagonal terms cancel in $B(a,b)-B(b,a)$, while the remaining
terms give the off-diagonal part of $(a,b)$. The omitted diagonal
part is $2\sum_i a_ib_i$, which is zero modulo two. Hence the
cocycle commutator is $(-1)^{(a,b)}$. For the second identity,
\[
 B(a,-a)\equiv\sum_i a_i^2+\sum_{i>j}a_i a_jG_{ij}
                  =\frac{(a,a)}2\pmod2.
\]
Evenness makes the last expression integral. In this root basis the
diagonal entries are two; an analogous formula in a general even
basis uses $G_{ii}/2$ in its diagonal exponent.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-lattice-2}: a norm-four exponential}
The opposite-momentum cocycle is $+1$. Expanding the exponential gives
\begin{align*}
 Y(e^a,z)e^{-a}={}&z^{-4}\one+z^{-3}a(-1)\one\\
 &+\frac{z^{-2}}2\bigl(a(-2)+a(-1)^2\bigr)\one\\
 &+z^{-1}\left(\frac{a(-3)}3+\frac{a(-1)a(-2)}2
                              +\frac{a(-1)^3}{6}\right)\one+O(1).
\end{align*}
The coefficient of $z^{-n-1}$ has weight $3-n$, since both input
states have weight two. Thus the four displayed coefficient weights
are $0,1,2,3$, exactly as required. There is no need to enumerate any
other lattice momentum because the output momentum is fixed at zero.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-lattice-3}: the affine share of $u$}
The two highest weights are $1$ and $13$, so
\[
 h_K=\frac{1\cdot3}{4(1+2)}
       +\frac{13\cdot15}{4(39+2)}
     =\frac14+\frac{195}{164}=\frac{59}{41}.
\]
The complementary weight is $2-59/41=23/41$. It is positive, as
it must be in the unitary complementary-stress decomposition.
This also checks the recorded pair
$180/1271+13/31=23/41$ for the two neutral stresses.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-lattice-4}: transport preserves unitarity}
Applying $\theta_g$ twice cancels the two oscillator signs, complex
conjugates the coefficients twice, and multiplies opposite momentum
factors $\prod_i\widetilde c_i^{\beta_i}$ and
$\prod_i\widetilde c_i^{-\beta_i}$. The result is the identity.
For $v\ne0$, invertibility of $g$ gives
$\langle v,v\rangle_g=\|g^{-1}v\|^2>0$.
Ordinary conjugation would omit the momentum-dependent torus factors.
For example the correct PCT of the raising sum uses the coefficients
$c_i$ in the lowering sum, rather than equal coefficients on all
opposite roots.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-lattice-5}: determinant and index}
If $L\subset\widetilde L$ has index $r$, a fundamental region of
$\widetilde L$ has volume $1/r$ times that of $L$. The Gram
determinant is the square of that volume, giving
$\det\widetilde L=\det L/r^2$. Here $r=3$ and $\det L=9$,
so the extended determinant is one. Isotropy is the condition that
the adjoined discriminant classes have integral conformal weights,
and hence that the overlattice remains even.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-1}: all negative modes}
Translation covariance gives $(Da)_{(n)}=-n a_{(n-1)}$.
Iterating at $n=-1$ gives
$(D^{r-1}a)_{(-1)}=(r-1)!a_{(-r)}$. Thus negative modes are
normal products with translated left inputs. Once translation, normal
products, and nonnegative modes preserve a space, every mode preserves
it, which is exactly vertex-subalgebra closure.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-2}: the missing lowest line}
Choose weight bases $v_0,\ldots,v_{m-1}$ and
$w_0,\ldots,w_{n-1}$ ordered from lowest to highest. The raising
image in the first factor contains every $v_i$ with $i>0$, and
the second contains every $w_j$ with $j>0$. Their sum therefore
contains all tensor basis vectors except $v_0\otimes w_0$.
That vector cannot be in the image because neither raising operator
can reach a joint-lowest weight. The quotient is one dimensional.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-4}: specialization can lose rank}
The matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&p\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
has rank two over $\Q$ and rank one over $\mathbb F_p$.
A nonzero minor modulo $p$ was nonzero over $\Q$, but a vanishing
minor modulo $p$ may merely have been divisible by $p$. The scalar
residual $p$ is the simplest example of a nonzero integer residual
that reduces to zero. Thus lower rank certificates and exact
zero-residual certificates require different evidence.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-6}: why one induction index fails}
In the term $b_{(n)}(a_{(0)}t)$, a normal expression for
$a_{(0)}t$ may contain a single generator of weight $\wt(t)+1$.
The left input $b$ has weight $\wt(s)-1$, so the total input
weight is unchanged. Induction on total weight alone has made no
progress. The left weight has decreased, however, so lexicographic
induction applies. Composite and derivative terms are already dealt
with by filtered Wick and do not need this second index.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-1}: parafermion charges}
Substitution gives $c_{\mathrm{PF}_2}=1/2$,
$c_{\mathrm{PF}_3}=4/5$, and $c_{\mathrm{PF}_{13}}=8/5$.
The first is the Ising parafermion algebra. Notice that the
parafermion central charges approach two from below as $k$ tends
to infinity; proximity to two by itself therefore selects no
particular level or full theory.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-3}: the spin-three simple pole}
Skew symmetry gives
$a_{(0)}a=-a_{(0)}a+D(a_{(1)}a)-\frac12D^2(a_{(2)}a)
+\frac16D^3(a_{(3)}a)-\cdots$ for an even field $a$.
Use $a_{(1)}a=b_4$, $a_{(2)}a=DL$, $a_{(3)}a=2L$.
Derivatives of the scalar higher pole vanish. Thus
\[
 2a_{(0)}a=Db_4-\frac12D^3L+\frac13D^3L
            =Db_4-\frac16D^3L.
\]
Dividing by two gives the displayed self-row.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-4}: the two isotropic lines}
If $j=0$, isotropy forces $k=0$. Otherwise divide by $j^2$ to
obtain $(k/j)^2=-1$ in $\mathbb F_{13}$. The two roots are
$5$ and $-5$. Adjoining both lines would adjoin their span, which
is the entire two-dimensional discriminant group. It contains
$(1,0)$, whose quadratic value is $-1/13$, so such an extension
would not have integral vacuum grading.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-1}: the graded quotient}
For homogeneous $a,b$, the vector $a_{(-2)}b$ has weight
$\wt(a)+\wt(b)+1$. Hence $C_2(V)$ is a direct sum of homogeneous
subspaces. In finite-dimensional $V_n$ every subspace has an
orthogonal complement, and
$V_n/(C_2(V)\cap V_n)$ is represented by that complement.
This is finite-dimensional linear algebra grade by grade; no
assertion about Hilbert-space closure of the infinite direct sum
is needed.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-2}: the trace step}
A diagonalizable operator with nonnegative eigenvalues has trace the
sum of those eigenvalues. Zero trace forces each to vanish, so the
operator vanishes. Without diagonalizability,
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has
zero spectrum and trace but is nonzero. This is precisely why the
regular component top decomposition is used on a generalized
weight-zero space: nilpotence of the total energy alone is insufficient.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-4}: a nonsplit extension}
Let $A=\C[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$. Its regular module fits
into
$0\to\C\varepsilon\to A\to A/(\varepsilon)\to0$,
with both endpoints the simple module on which $\varepsilon$ acts
as zero. A section would send $1$ to $1+b\varepsilon$, but
$\varepsilon(1+b\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\ne0$, so no section is
$A$-linear. The simple module is therefore not projective. In the
VOA proof, the positive stress identity supplies the missing
vacuum-like lift and thereby rules out the analogous nonsplit
extension onto the unit.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-5}: factorial versus exponential}
For $n$ even, the final $n/2$ factors of $n!$ are at least $n/2$,
so $n!\geq(n/2)^{n/2}$. Once $n/2>A^2$, this is greater than
$A^n$. A fixed prefactor can be absorbed by choosing $n$ larger.
The odd case follows from the neighboring even case or the same
estimate with integer parts.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-1}: the $x_4$ top}
At $m=6$, the lattice--Potts contribution is $36/156=3/13$.
The paired parafermion charge is $k=-5\cdot6=-4$ modulo thirteen,
and its energy is
$4\cdot5/15-16/13=4/39$. The sum is
$9/39+4/39=1/3$. The opposite charge supplies the conjugate
minimizer, accounting for the two-dimensional top.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-3}: multiplicities are squared}
Schur's lemma gives $\Hom(X_i,X_j)=0$ for $i\ne j$ and
$\End(X_i)=\C$. Hence the endomorphism algebra is
$\bigoplus_iM_{m_i}(\C)$ and has dimension $\sum_i m_i^2$.
The branching $1+X+Y+Y^*$ therefore gives rank four if the four
simples differ, and rank six when it becomes $1+X+2Y$.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-4}: the complex fusion algebra}
In a one-dimensional representation, the cross relation forces
$\alpha=1$ when $\rho\ne0$. The quadratic then has roots $d_m$ and
$-d_m^{-1}$. For the two-dimensional representation take
\[
 \alpha=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_3&0\\0&\zeta_3^2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The cross relation is immediate, and
$(1+\alpha+\alpha^2)\rho=0$, so the quadratic relation reduces to
$\rho^2=1$. These inequivalent irreducibles have squared dimensions
$1+1+4=6$, exhausting the six-dimensional semisimple complex fusion
algebra.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-6}: center global dimension}
The sum is
\[
 1+d^4+4(1+d^2)^2+6(3d)^2.
\]
The relation $d^2=1+3d$ gives $d^4-11d^2+1=0$. Subtracting
the displayed sum from $9(1+d^2)^2$ gives
$4(d^4-11d^2+1)=0$. Thus the center global dimension is the square
of the boundary global dimension $3(1+d^2)$. Its total quantum
dimension is the positive square root, namely $3(1+d^2)$. Its
rank is twelve; these are three different invariants.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-4}: solving for a Virasoro axis}
Expand $e*e$ using the table. The coefficient of $e_i$ is
$2a_i^2+t^2\lambda_i/f_i$ and that of $x$ is
$2t\lambda\cdot a$. Equating them to $2a_i$ and $2t$ gives
$a_i(1-a_i)=\lambda_i t^2/(2f_i)$ and the second equation.
Substituting the three pairs $\lambda_i,f_i$ gives the stated $q_i$.
Let $\omega'=e_0+e_1+e_2$. Invariance and $\omega'*e=2e$
give
$2\langle e,e\rangle=\langle\omega',e*e\rangle
=2\langle\omega',e\rangle$. Therefore
$c(e)=2\langle e,e\rangle=2\sum_i f_i a_i$ on the
idempotent locus.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-5}: the vacuum grade-four Gram matrix}
Using $[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\frac c{12}(m^3-m)\delta_{m+n,0}$
and $L_{-1}\one=0$, the matrix in the stated basis is
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}5c&3c\\3c&c(c+8)/2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
For example the mixed entry follows from
$L_4L_{-2}^2\one=6L_2L_{-2}\one=3c\one$.
Its determinant is
$5c^2(c+8)/2-9c^2=c^2(5c+22)/2$, positive for $c>0$.
The two vacuum descendants are therefore independent at $c_*$.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-7}: bounded projected energy}
Project $(m,n)\in\Z^2$ to $m+\theta n$ with irrational $\theta$.
There are infinitely many pairs whose projected value lies in any
fixed sufficiently large interval: for each $n$, choose $m$ nearest
to $-\theta n$, giving absolute value at most $1/2$. Their full
Euclidean lengths grow, so a finite ambient-energy cutoff would
remove all but finitely many. Discarding the perpendicular energy
removes that control. This example illustrates the convergence
problem; it does not identify the actual irrational commutant
branching with a freely projected lattice.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-8}: a sum and its diagonal square}
The example $a_n=1/n$ has a divergent sum and a convergent square
sum. Therefore the divergence of $R(\beta)$ alone would be
insufficient. A character is more special: write it as a sum over
an actual basis,
$\chi_\lambda(\beta)=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}$, including repetitions
for multiplicities. Its square is a sum over ordered pairs of basis
vectors. Keeping only the pairs $(j,j)$ gives
$\chi_\lambda(\beta)^2\geq\sum_j e^{-2\beta E_j}
=\chi_\lambda(2\beta)$.
All terms are positive, and integer multiplicities permit this
basis interpretation. Since $R(2\beta)$ diverges for every
$\beta>0$, the diagonal thermal sum diverges too.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-10}: the dimension threshold}
For a noninvertible simple $X$, the product $X\otimes X^*$ contains
the unit and at least one further simple. All simple
Frobenius--Perron dimensions are at least one, so
$\FPdim(X)^2\geq2$. If there are at least three simples, two
others each contribute at least one to global dimension, proving
the bound four.

At rank two, the nonunit simple is self-dual and
$X^2=\one+nX$ for an integer $n\geq1$. Its positive dimension
is $(n+\sqrt{n^2+4})/2$, an increasing function of $n$. At $n=1$
the global dimension is $1+((1+\sqrt5)/2)^2=(5+\sqrt5)/2$.
In the descent argument, a nontrivial central adjoint algebra would
have dimension at least $d^2$, forcing the image category below
both nonpointed thresholds. Its being pointed would contradict the
irrational dimension of the image of $\rho$.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-3}: counting the raising quotient}
The summands whose left representation has dimension one contribute
\[
 3+1+5+2+3+1+2+1=18
\]
irreducible constituents and
\[
 9+5+35+18+33+13+30+19=162
\]
dimensions. Those whose left representation has dimension two
contribute $1+1+2+2+2+1+1=10$ constituents and
$4+8+24+32+40+24+28=160$ dimensions. Thus $P$ has dimension
$322$ and $28$ constituents, with multiplicity. Each constituent
contributes precisely one joint-lowest vector to the quotient by
$E_LP+E_RP$, by Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-2}. The image therefore
has dimension $322-28=294$. Multiplicity copies require separate
seeds even when their representation types agree.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-closure-5}: the positive-mode bootstrap}
For a weight-two state, the Virasoro commutator formula gives
\[
 [L_1,Y(a,z)]=z^2\partial_zY(a,z)+4zY(a,z)+Y(L_1a,z).
\]
Comparing the coefficient of $z^{-n-1}$ yields
\[
 [L_1,a_{(n)}]=(2-n)a_{(n+1)}+(L_1a)_{(n)}.
\]
Quasiprimary means $L_1a=0$, so the last term vanishes. Apply
the $n=0$ identity to $p$ and rearrange:
\[
 2a_{(1)}p=L_1(a_{(0)}p)-a_{(0)}L_1p.
\]
Thus control of the zero product, together with the already proved
$L_1$ stability of its output space, controls the next positive
mode. Quasiprimary does not mean that $L_2a$ vanishes; this is why
the subsequent $L_2$ argument separately keeps track of scalar terms.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-2}: three opposite momentum pairs}
Using $r^2=13$, $t^2=-3$, and $\overline t=-t$, the three products are
\[
 p_{\beta_1}p_{-\beta_1}
 =p_{\beta_3}p_{-\beta_3}
 =\frac{r^2-9t^2}{1000}
 =\frac{40}{1000}=\frac1{25},
\]
and
\[
 p_{\beta_2}p_{-\beta_2}
 =\frac{49-9r^2t^2}{10000}
 =\frac{49+351}{10000}=\frac1{25}.
\]
The transported PCT multiplies an opposite exponential by
$10,1,10$ for the three positive momenta. Directly,
\begin{align*}
 10\overline{p_{\beta_1}}&=(-r+3t)/10=p_{-\beta_1},\\
 \overline{p_{\beta_2}}&=(7-3rt)/100=p_{-\beta_2},\\
 10\overline{p_{\beta_3}}&=-(r+3t)/10=p_{-\beta_3}.
\end{align*}
The opposite directions use the reciprocal factors, so each
two-term exponential pair is fixed. Each Heisenberg oscillator
changes sign under PCT; its quadratic products are fixed as well.
Consequently the entire Potts stress is PCT real in the transported
positive form.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-5}: coefficients of the stress identity}
In the basis $(\omega_L,\omega_R,e,f)$, the four coefficients on
the proposed right side are
\begin{align*}
 \omega_L &: 1,\\
 \omega_R &: \frac{3403}{4563}+\frac5{27}+\frac{35}{507}
             =\frac{3403+845+315}{4563}=1,\\
 e &: \frac38+\frac58=1,\\
 f &: \frac{13}{14}+\frac1{14}=1.
\end{align*}
They therefore reproduce the sum of the four orthogonal stresses.
The norm of a vector gives only one quadratic constraint; it
does not determine these four projection coefficients. More
fundamentally, a correct central term is only one pole in a
self-OPE. Establishing the Potts or parafermion subalgebra also
requires the remaining singular products, the requisite higher
fields, and the stated translation and PCT properties.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-factors-6}: positivity without commutativity}
Take
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Their spectra are $\{1,2\}$ and $\{1,3\}$, respectively, so both
are positive definite and self-adjoint. But
$[A,B]=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
For $\alpha,\beta>0$ and every vector $v$,
\[
 \langle(\alpha A+\beta B)v,v\rangle
 \leq(2\alpha+3\beta)\|v\|^2.
\]
This follows by bounding each expectation separately and adding.
No common eigenbasis was used. The $C_2$ proof does the same
thing with the partial energies on a fixed ambient grade: it bounds
each expectation on the same vector in $C_2(V)^\perp$, then uses
the positive stress identity to bound the total energy.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-3}: downward induction to the vacuum condition}
For each fixed $a$, lower truncation supplies an integer $N$ with
$a_{(n)}v=0$ for all $n\geq N$. Since $L_{-1}v=0$,
translation covariance implies
$(n+1)a_{(n)}v=-L_{-1}a_{(n+1)}v$.
Descending from $N$ first reaches $a_{(3)}v=0$, and then gives
\[
 3a_{(2)}v=-L_{-1}a_{(3)}v=0,\qquad
 2a_{(1)}v=-L_{-1}a_{(2)}v=0,\qquad
 a_{(0)}v=-L_{-1}a_{(1)}v=0.
\]
The coefficient is nonzero for every $n\geq0$. At $n=-1$ it
would vanish, exactly where a creation mode may act nontrivially.
In particular the argument imposes no erroneous vanishing of
$a_{(-1)}v$, which is the desired copy of the state $a$.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-regular-6}: the logical uses of the hypotheses}
Positive coefficients let separate upper bounds on partial-energy
expectations add to an upper bound on ambient energy. They also
make a zero sum of nonnegative partial traces force each trace
to vanish. Component regularity supplies ordinary semisimple
restrictions, finitely many simple types, and, together with its
$C_2$ consequences, the finite component quotient bounds.
Grading compatibility makes component modes have their expected
ambient degrees. It places a lowest ambient space in complete
component tops and makes each partial energy preserve finite
ambient grades.

The unique simple component module at weight zero identifies those
zero-energy tops with vacuum tops. Their translation operators
therefore annihilate the selected vectors, giving the vacuum-like
lift needed for projectivity. If another simple had weight zero,
the trace argument would still force its energy to vanish, but
would not force its translation mode to vanish. Both uniqueness
of the $V$ module of weight zero and vacuum projectivity would
lose their stated proof. The $C_2$ bound itself would remain valid
if the other component hypotheses remained true.

Braiding is a premise of the categorical rigidity theorem used after
projectivity. Before rigidity, right exactness turns a surjection
$P^{\oplus d_0(Z)}\twoheadrightarrow Z$ into one onto $Z\otimes X$;
this gives the exponential bound on tensor-power endomorphisms.
Only after rigidity has been established is tensoring known to be
exact, allowing projectivity of the unit to imply projectivity of
every object. Interchanging these stages would be circular.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-2}: checking the top certificate}
The coefficient $4680(17/10)$ equals $7956$. For the requested
three variables the left-side coefficients are
\[
 x_0:7956,\qquad
 x_5:14(7956)-148560=-37176,\qquad
 y_4:14(7956)-81480=29904.
\]
The right side gives respectively $7956$,
$-8460-28716=-37176$, and $28716+1188=29904$.
In particular the negative $x_5$ coefficient is absorbed by
the actual representation constraints
$y_2-x_5\geq0$ and $y_4-x_5-x_6\geq0$.

Ignoring those constraints and taking a weighted average of
individual row ratios gives only the bound by their maximum.
The $x_5$ row alone has ratio $619/273>2$. Such a bound could
not exclude an additional integer-graded module beginning at
weight two. The current character inequalities force the high
Cartan charges of that row to come with lower-charge companions;
their combined contribution yields the stronger bound $17/10$.
The individual ratio is bookkeeping, not a separate ambient
energy eigenvalue.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-branch-5}: the arithmetic exclusion}
Expanding the difference gives
\begin{align*}
 36m^2(9m^2+4)-13(4m^2+2)^2
 &=116m^4-64m^2-52\\
 &=4(m^2-1)(29m^2+13).
\end{align*}
For $m>1$ it is strictly positive. Both sides of the preceding
root-sum inequality are positive, so squaring would give the
opposite weak inequality; this is the contradiction outside
the exceptional squareclass.

For $m=2$, $\sqrt{9m^2+4}=2\sqrt{10}$, and the second moment is
\[
 \frac{-4+(2\varepsilon-2)2\sqrt{10}}2
 =\begin{cases}
 -2,&\varepsilon=1,\\
 -2-4\sqrt{10},&\varepsilon=-1.
 \end{cases}
\]
The first sum is $-2-2\sqrt{10}$, expressed with $4m^2+2=18$
roots. The conductor-forty bound $a+8b$ is exactly
$2+8(2)=18$, so its equality condition requires the squared-root
sum to be $a=2$. Neither permitted indicator sign gives that value.
Thus the exceptional case does not survive.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-1}: the perpendicular lattice}
The orthogonal summands make the glue norm the sum
$2/3+2/3+1/2+78/36=4$. Its components have orders
$3,3,2,6$ modulo their respective lattices, so the full glue
class has order $\operatorname{lcm}(3,3,2,6)=6$.
Its pairings with the original lattice are integral: the
$A_2$ weights are dual-lattice vectors, while
$(\alpha/2,\alpha)=1$ and $(u_0/6,u_0)=13$.
The even norm therefore gives an even integral index-six extension.
Its determinant is
\[
 \frac{3\cdot3\cdot2\cdot78}{6^2}
 =\frac{1404}{36}=39.
\]
If the plane lattice $L_0$ had a larger saturation of index
$r>1$ in $E_8$, that integral lattice would have determinant
$39/r^2$. Integrality of its Gram matrix makes this an integer,
so $r^2$ would divide the squarefree integer $39$, impossible.
This argument uses the actual integral ambient lattice; an
arbitrary real enlargement has no such determinant restriction.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-2}: why branching multiplicities square}
For each lattice charge $n$, the three nonzero branching functions
are $\chi_n,\chi_n,2\chi_n$. Pairing each full multiplicity
space with its complex conjugate produces multiplicities
$1^2,1^2,2^2$. Summing gives $6Z_{L_0}$. The lattice vacuum
occurs at $n=0$, so the zero-energy multiplicity is six.

A partition function alone supplies no product on that
six-dimensional vacuum space. A full interpretation requires
a unital associative commutative vacuum product, its positive pairing and
adjoint, and compatible local OPE and sewing data for all other
sectors. In a unitary finite vacuum algebra the mutually orthogonal
primitive idempotents describe the separate vacuum components;
defect junctions must act consistently on this algebra. Six
disconnected identical lattice theories give one possible partition
function with this multiplicity, but do not automatically supply
the intended Haagerup action. A unique-vacuum, clustering theory
cannot simply keep the six-dimensional zero-energy space unchanged.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-3}: exact near-two charges}
The affine charge is $3(40)/(40+2)=20/7$. Hence the diagonal
GKO charge is
\[
 1+\frac{117}{41}-\frac{20}{7}
 =\frac{286}{287}=1-\frac1{287}.
\]
Adding one Cartan stress gives $573/287=2-1/287$.
Its distance below two is $1/287$, approximately $.00348432$,
whereas $c_*-2$ is approximately $.025004294$ and is positive.
For completeness, the GKO stress by itself is $288/287$ below
two, and the diagonal affine stress is $6/7$ above two.
These exact rational stresses and the irrational axis are different
vectors and different chiral algebras; closeness of their charges
does not identify their full theories.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-6}: the trivial grading}
Let $g_X$ be the group element assigned to a simple $X$, with
$g_Z=g_Xg_Y$ whenever $Z$ occurs in $X\otimes Y$.
Since both $\one$ and $\rho$ occur in $\rho^2$,
$g_\rho^2=1=g_\rho$. Thus $g_\rho=1$.
The constituent $\alpha\rho$ then implies
$g_\alpha g_\rho=g_{\alpha\rho}=g_\rho^2=1$,
so $g_\alpha=1$; the same holds for every remaining simple.
The group need not be abelian for this argument.

To apply this to currents, each simple line must transmit the
chosen current algebra by a definite invertible rotation, and
every nonzero fusion junction must identify the rotation of
each output constituent with the product of the two input
rotations. Those rotations then give the required group-valued
assignment. Preserving only the stress, or retaining arbitrary
matrix elements of a line after a projection, does not establish
this transmission property.

\subsection*{Exercise~\ref{ex:c-coset-9}: independent temperatures}
Write $s=\beta+\bar\beta$, $p=\beta\bar\beta$, and
$t=4\pi^2s/p$. The inverted temperatures sum to $t$, while
eta inversion gives
\[
 \eta(4\pi^2/\beta)\eta(4\pi^2/\bar\beta)
 =\frac{\sqrt p}{2\pi}\eta(\beta)\eta(\bar\beta).
\]
Equating the original and inverted amplitudes therefore gives
$G(t)=\sqrt p\,G(s)/(2\pi)=\sqrt{s/t}\,G(s)$.
For fixed $s>0$, independently positive temperatures allow
all $0<p\leq s^2/4$, equivalently all $t$ with
$st\geq16\pi^2$. Given $s_1,s_2>0$, choose
$u\geq16\pi^2\max(s_1^{-1},s_2^{-1})$. Both pairs $(s_i,u)$
are allowed, forcing $\sqrt{s_1}G(s_1)=\sqrt{s_2}G(s_2)$.

If only equal temperatures were checked, one would have
$p=s^2/4$ and only the involution $t=16\pi^2/s$.
It relates each argument to one partner, with no freedom to
connect two arbitrary arguments through a common $u$.
An invariance under this involution alone does not force
$\sqrt{s}G(s)$ to be constant. The stronger two-variable
modularity hypothesis is essential to the uniqueness proof.
